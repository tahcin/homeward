# Homeward: evidence file

Checked 2026-10-15. I opened every source listed here (abstract or full text, through PubMed records served by Europe PMC, PMC, journal pages, or agency PDFs) unless an item says otherwise. Figures are quoted as the source gives them. "Strength" is a plain judgement: study design, size, and how far the number can be stretched.

Rules for using this file:

- Quote the population and the measure along with the number. "78% of ED patients misunderstood at least one area" is accurate. "78% of patients don't understand their instructions" is not.
- Items marked NOT VERIFIED stay off slides until someone opens the primary source.
- Several of the strongest studies are US-based and English-only. Say "in a US study" when the setting matters.

## 1. Patients do not understand or remember discharge instructions

**1.1 Most ED patients leave with a gap in understanding, and most don't know it.**
- Figure: "Seventy-eight percent of patients demonstrated deficient comprehension (less than complete concordance) in at least 1 domain; 51% of patients, in 2 or more domains." Also: "Patients perceived difficulty with comprehension only 20% of the time when they demonstrated deficient comprehension."
- Source: Engel KG et al. Patient comprehension of emergency department care and instructions: are patients aware of when they do not understand? Ann Emerg Med 2009;53(4):454-461. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18619710/
- n: 140 adult English-speaking patients or caregivers, 2 US health systems.
- Strength: moderate. Small, observational, but rigorous (patient recall checked against the chart by two raters). The four domains were diagnosis, ED care, post-ED care and return instructions. "Deficient" means anything short of complete concordance, so it includes small gaps. The 20% figure is the best evidence that asking "do you understand?" doesn't work. That is the case for teach-back.

**1.2 Home care and "when to come back" instructions are the worst understood.**
- Figure: "Knowledge deficits were demonstrated by the majority of patients in the domain of home care instructions (80%) and return instructions (79%). Less frequent deficits were found for the domains of follow-up (39%), medications (22%), and diagnosis (14%)." Minimal or no understanding "was found in 40% of cases for home care and 51% for return instructions."
- Source: Engel KG et al. Patient understanding of emergency department discharge instructions: where are knowledge deficits greatest? Acad Emerg Med 2012;19(9):1035-1044. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22978730/
- n: 159 adult English-speaking patients, phone interview 24-36 hours after ED discharge, 5 diagnoses.
- Strength: moderate. Note: idea.md quotes 40.1% and 50.7%. The abstract says 40% and 51%. Use the abstract figures unless someone checks the full-text table.

**1.3 Across 51 studies, patients correctly recall only about half of what they are told at ED discharge.**
- Figure: pooled correct recall "47% for patients who received verbal information (95% CI 32.2% to 61.7%), 58% for patients who received written information (95% CI 44.2% to 71.2%), and 67% for patients who received video information."
- Source: Hoek AE et al. Patient discharge instructions in the emergency department and their effects on comprehension and recall of discharge instructions: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Ann Emerg Med 2020;75(3):435-444. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31439363/
- n: 51 studies; meta-analysis on 1,460 (verbal), 3,395 (written) and 459 (video) patients.
- Strength: strong as a summary (systematic review with meta-analysis), though included studies are heterogeneous.

**1.4 Older inpatients say they understand, but fewer than half recall their follow-up appointment.**
- Figure: "Although 349 patients (95.6%) reported understanding the reason they had been in the hospital, only 218 patients (59.6%) were able to accurately describe their diagnosis." Of patients with a scheduled appointment, "54 (43.9%) accurately recalled details of either appointment."
- Source: Horwitz LI et al. Quality of discharge practices and patient understanding at an academic medical center. JAMA Intern Med 2013;173(18):1715-1722. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23958851/
- n: 395 patients aged 65+ (heart attack, heart failure, pneumonia), one US academic centre.
- Strength: moderate. Good for "self-rated understanding is not real understanding."

**1.5 Medication changes at discharge are poorly understood, and reconciliation errors are common.**
- Figure: "patients had no understanding of 142/205 (69.3 %) of redosed medications, 182/223 (81.6 %) of stopped medications, and 493 (62.0 %) of new medications. Altogether, 307 patients (81.4 %) either experienced a provider error, or had no understanding of at least one intended medication change." Also 137 of 565 changed medications (24.2%) were suspected provider errors.
- Source: Ziaeian B et al. Medication reconciliation accuracy and patient understanding of intended medication changes on hospital discharge. J Gen Intern Med 2012;27(11):1513-1520. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22798200/
- n: 377 patients aged 65+, one US academic centre.
- Strength: moderate. Very relevant to Homeward: the doses and stop/start changes are exactly what the fact lock protects and the teach-back checks.

**1.6 "40-80% of medical information is forgotten immediately" (use with care).**
- Figure: "40-80% of medical information provided by healthcare practitioners is forgotten immediately" and "almost half of the information that is remembered is incorrect."
- Source: Kessels RP. Patients' memory for medical information. J R Soc Med 2003;96(5):219-222. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC539473/
- n: none (narrative review).
- Strength: weak for a slide. In the full text the 40-80% sentence has no citation attached, and the "almost half is incorrect" line comes from a 1979 rheumatology clinic study (Anderson et al.). Widely quoted, but the figure is not traceable to one dataset. Prefer 1.3 (Hoek 2020) for recall.

## 2. Teach-back: does it work, and do clinicians do it?

**2.1 In an ED cohort, teach-back cut the share of patients leaving with a comprehension gap from 49% to 11.9%, and was faster than the usual talk.**
- Figure: "After teach-back, the proportion of patients that left the ED with a comprehension deficit declined from 49 to 11.9%. Deficits were most common for return precautions in both groups (41.3% versus 8.1%). Teach-back conversation took 1:39 min, versus an average of 3:11 min for a regular discharge interview."
- Source: Mahajan M et al. The impact of teach-back on patient recall and understanding of discharge information in the emergency department: the Emergency Teach-Back (EM-TeBa) study. Int J Emerg Med 2020;13:49. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7513274/
- n: 483 (239 control, 244 teach-back), one non-academic ED in the Netherlands.
- Strength: moderate. Prospective cohort, not randomised. Strong for the time argument (teach-back does not have to be slow) and directly about discharge.

**2.2 Newest meta-analysis (2026): teach-back improves adherence and self-efficacy, but not clearly knowledge.**
- Figure: 18 RCTs, 1,985 participants. "Overall, there was no clear pattern of the effect of teach-back on knowledge (very low COE)." Adherence (7 RCTs, 571 participants): "SMD = 1.04; 95%CI 0.45-1.64) (low COE)." Self-efficacy (5 RCTs, 416 participants): "SMD = 2.40; 95%CI 0.37-4.44) (very low COE)."
- Source: Vick JB et al. Teach-Back in Clinical Communication: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis. J Gen Intern Med 2026. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11606-026-10678-y (PubMed 42693371)
- Strength: strongest design, weakest certainty. This is the honest counterweight. Don't claim "teach-back is proven to improve knowledge." Safe claim: teach-back is the recommended way to check understanding, and trials show better adherence. Homeward's core value is detection (telling the nurse which fact was missed), which does not depend on teach-back itself raising knowledge scores.

**2.3 Teach-back with discharge education cut heart-failure readmissions in a meta-analysis.**
- Figure: "discharge education using TBM significantly reduced the overall readmission rates (odds ratio = 0.40, 95% confidence interval 0.17-0.94)."
- Source: Oh S et al. Effectiveness of discharge education using teach-back method on readmission among heart failure patients: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Patient Educ Couns 2023;107:107559. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36411152/
- n: 7 studies reviewed, 6 meta-analysed; quality varied, only two at low risk of bias.
- Strength: moderate to weak. Heart failure only; wide confidence interval.

**2.4 Earlier reviews: positive but inconsistent.**
- Ha Dinh TT et al. JBI Database System Rev Implement Rep 2016;14(1):210-247. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26878928/ 12 studies, chronic disease. "Five studies found a reduction in readmission rates and hospitalization but these were not always statistically significant." No meta-analysis possible. Strength: moderate, narrative.
- Talevski J et al. Teach-back: a systematic review of implementation and impacts. PLoS One 2020;15(4):e0231350. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32287296/ "Use of teach-back proved effective in 19 of the 20 studies." Strength: moderate (20 studies of mixed quality, four rated high).

**2.5 Clinicians rarely do it: in one observed study, 1 patient in 33 was asked to teach back.**
- Figure: "only 1 patient was asked to teach back his understanding of the discharge plan." Also "Most patients (27 of 33, 81%) did not receive guidance on red-flag signs" and of 30 patients with a medication change, "8 (27%) received no verbal instruction on the change."
- Source: Trivedi SP et al. Assessment of Patient Education Delivered at Time of Hospital Discharge. JAMA Intern Med 2023;183(5):417-423. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36939674/
- n: 33 patients directly observed from 7 am to discharge, 2 US teaching hospitals (2018-2019).
- Strength: moderate. Small but the method (a trained observer at the bedside all day) is the best evidence on what really happens. 6 of 33 patients preferred Spanish.

**2.6 Older evidence that checking understanding is rare.**
- Schillinger D et al. Closing the loop: physician communication with diabetic patients who have low health literacy. Arch Intern Med 2003;163(1):83-90. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12523921/ "Physicians assessed recall and comprehension of any new concept in 12 (20%) of 61 visits and for 15 (12%) of 124 new concepts." n = 38 physicians, 74 patients. Outpatient, not discharge.
- Rhodes KV et al. Ann Emerg Med 2004;44(3):262-267. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15332069/ ED discharge instructions "averaged 76 seconds (range 7 to 202 seconds)" and "there were no instances in which the provider confirmed patient understanding." n = 31 audiotaped discharges. Old and small; use as colour only.

**2.7 AHRQ recommends teach-back with every patient and says it need not lengthen a visit.**
- Quote: teach-back is "a way of checking understanding by asking patients to state in their own words what they need to know or do about their health." "Do you understand?" and "Does that make sense?" "are NOT teach-back questions." Advice: "use teach-back with everyone, every time" and once routine it "can be done without awkwardness and does not lengthen a visit." Also recommends "Chunk and Check" and repeating the re-teach cycle until the patient explains correctly.
- Source: AHRQ Health Literacy Universal Precautions Toolkit, 3rd Edition, Tool 5: Use the Teach-Back Method. https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool5.html
- Strength: authoritative guidance, not trial data. The page gives no minutes figure. Homeward's flow (chunked questions, re-teach, ask again) mirrors this tool exactly, which is worth saying.

## 3. Adverse events and medication errors after discharge

**3.1 About one in five medical patients has an adverse event after going home; two thirds are drug-related.**
- Figure: "Seventy-six patients had adverse events after discharge (19% [95% CI, 15% to 23%])." "Adverse drug events were the most common type of adverse event (66% [CI, 55% to 76%])." Preventable: 6%; ameliorable: 6%.
- Source: Forster AJ et al. The incidence and severity of adverse events affecting patients after discharge from the hospital. Ann Intern Med 2003;138(3):161-167. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12558354/
- n: 400 consecutive patients, one US tertiary hospital, phone follow-up about 3 weeks after discharge.
- Strength: moderate to strong (prospective, physician-adjudicated), but old and single-centre. Correct phrasing: "19% (95% CI 15-23%)", not "19-23%".

**3.2 Replication in Canada: 23%, and half preventable or ameliorable.**
- Figure: "76 of the 328 patients experienced at least 1 AE (overall incidence 23%, 95% confidence interval [CI] 19%-28%)." "The most common AEs were adverse drug events (72%)." "half of the AEs were preventable or ameliorable."
- Source: Forster AJ et al. Adverse events among medical patients after discharge from hospital. CMAJ 2004;170(3):345-349. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14757670/
- n: 328 patients, median age 71.
- Strength: moderate.

**3.3 Newer data: unintentional medication discrepancies at discharge in half of critically ill older adults, doubling ED visits.**
- Figure: "68% encountered unintentional medication discrepancies at some point during care transitions, with prevalence of 35% at admission, 20% during transfer, and 49% at discharge." Discrepancies were linked to "a twofold higher risk of ED visits within 30 days of discharge (HR = 2.13, 95% CI = 1.06-4.30)."
- Source: Park J et al. BMC Geriatr 2024;24:1000. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39696013/
- n: 339 patients aged 65+, ED-ICU, Korea, retrospective.
- Strength: moderate to weak (retrospective, single setting). Shows the problem persists in recent data.

See also 1.5 (Ziaeian 2012): 24.2% of medication changes at discharge were suspected provider errors.

## 4. Readmissions: size, cost, penalties, preventability

**4.1 US 30-day all-cause readmission rate and cost (latest HCUP brief found).**
- Figure: "From 2016 to 2020, the 30-day all-cause hospital readmission rate remained at 13.9 per 100 index admissions." Table 1: 3,850 thousand readmissions in 2020 (all payers). Medicare rate 17.0 per 100. "On average, the cost of readmissions in 2020 was 12.4 percent higher than the cost of index admissions ($16,300 vs. $14,500)."
- Source: Jiang HJ, Hensche MK. Characteristics of 30-Day All-Cause Hospital Readmissions, 2016-2020. HCUP Statistical Brief #304. AHRQ, September 2023. https://hcup-us.ahrq.gov/reports/statbriefs/sb304-readmissions-2016-2020.jsp (the live site timed out from here; I read the Internet Archive copy of the same page).
- Strength: strong (national all-payer database).
- Derived, our arithmetic, label it as an estimate: 3.85 million readmissions x $16,300 average is roughly $63 billion in 2020. HCUP does not state that total in the brief.
- NOT VERIFIED: a newer HCUP brief (SB307, 2020 conditions by payer) exists, but I could not open it.

**4.2 Medicare penalises 80% of evaluated hospitals for excess readmissions in fiscal 2027.**
- Figure: "Eighty percent of hospitals, or 2,334 facilities, are having their Medicare payments reduced," up from 78% (2,304) in FY2026. Max penalty 3%; only 10 hospitals at the maximum. Reported elsewhere (Becker's, relayed by KFF Health News morning briefing): 80.2% of 2,912 evaluated hospitals, average penalty 0.44%, 578 hospitals not penalised. First cycle to include Medicare Advantage patients; uses July 2023 to June 2025 data.
- Source: Healthcare Dive, 8 Oct 2026, reporting new CMS data. https://www.healthcaredive.com/news/hospital-readmission-reduction-program-penalties-rise-2027-cms/832473/ . CMS programme page (3% cap; six measures: heart attack, COPD, heart failure, pneumonia, CABG, elective hip/knee replacement): https://www.cms.gov/medicare/payment/prospective-payment-systems/acute-inpatient-pps/hospital-readmissions-reduction-program-hrrp
- Strength: reputable secondary reporting of CMS data; the 3% cap is primary. NOT VERIFIED: total dollar value of FY2026 or FY2027 penalties (I found no current figure; older KFF reports of about $563 million are for FY2020 and should not be used).

**4.3 About a quarter of readmissions are judged potentially preventable.**
- Figure: "Of these, 269 (26.9%) were considered potentially preventable." Among the common factors: "patient lack of awareness of whom to contact after discharge (affecting 6.2%)" and "inability to keep appointments after discharge (affecting 8.3%)."
- Source: Auerbach AD et al. Preventability and Causes of Readmissions in a National Cohort of General Medicine Patients. JAMA Intern Med 2016;176(4):484-493. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26954564/
- n: 1,000 readmitted patients, 12 US academic centres.
- Strength: strong for this question (multi-perspective review). Agrees with the systematic review below.
- Supporting: van Walraven C et al. CMAJ 2011;183(7):E391-402. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21444623/ "The median proportion of readmissions deemed avoidable was 27.1% but varied from 5% to 79%." 34 studies, mostly subjective criteria.

**4.4 A discharge package with patient education cut 30-day hospital use by about 30% (Project RED).**
- Figure: intervention group had "a lower rate of hospital utilization than those receiving usual care (0.314 vs. 0.451 visit per person per month; incidence rate ratio, 0.695 [95% CI, 0.515 to 0.937]; P = 0.009)."
- Source: Jack BW et al. A reengineered hospital discharge program to decrease rehospitalization: a randomized trial. Ann Intern Med 2009;150(3):178-187. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19189907/
- n: 749 randomised (370 vs 368 analysed), English-speaking, one US safety-net hospital.
- Strength: strong (RCT) for the bundle. The bundle had several parts (nurse advocate, booklet, pharmacist call), so the gain can't be credited to education alone.

## 5. Language barriers

**5.1 Interpreter phones at every bedside cut readmissions for limited-English patients from 17.8% to 13.4%.**
- Figure: "There was a significant decrease in observed 30-day readmission rates for the LEP group during the 8-month intervention period compared with 18 months preintervention (17.8% vs. 13.4%); at the same time English proficient readmission rates increased (16.7% vs. 19.7%)." The gain "was not maintained during the subsequent postintervention period when the telephones became less accessible." Estimated "119 readmissions averted" and "monthly hospital expenditure savings of $161,404."
- Source: Karliner LS, Pérez-Stable EJ, Gregorich SE. Convenient Access to Professional Interpreters in the Hospital Decreases Readmission Rates and Estimated Hospital Expenditures for Patients With Limited English Proficiency. Med Care 2017;55(3):199-206. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27579909/
- n: 8,077 discharges (1,963 LEP), patients aged 50+, one US academic medicine floor; natural experiment.
- Strength: moderate. Not randomised, single site, but has a comparison group and a "dose-response" in time (benefit faded when access dropped). Intervention was dual-handset interpreter telephones, not in-person interpreters.

**5.2 Limited-English patients understand their discharge medications less well.**
- Figure: "Rates of understanding were low overall for follow-up appointment type (56%) and the 3 medication outcomes (category 48%, purpose 55%, both 41%)." LEP vs English-proficient: appointment type 50% vs 66%; medication category 45% vs 54%. Adjusted odds of understanding medication category: OR 0.63 (95% CI 0.42-0.95).
- Source: Karliner LS et al. Language barriers and understanding of hospital discharge instructions. Med Care 2012;50(4):283-289. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22411441/
- n: 308 patients (203 LEP; Spanish, Chinese, English speakers), 2 US hospitals.
- Strength: moderate.

**5.3 Adverse events in limited-English patients more often cause physical harm, and more often stem from communication errors.**
- Figure: "About 49.1% of limited English proficient patient adverse events involved some physical harm whereas only 29.5% of adverse events for patients who speak English resulted in physical harm." Harm moderate or worse: 46.8% vs 24.4%. Caused by communication errors: 52.4% vs 35.9%.
- Source: Divi C et al. Language proficiency and adverse events in US hospitals: a pilot study. Int J Qual Health Care 2007;19(2):60-67. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17277013/
- n: adverse event reports from 6 Joint Commission-accredited hospitals over 7 months in 2005 (abstract gives no event count).
- Strength: weak to moderate. A pilot using incident reports; it compares the nature of events, not the rate of events. Correct phrasing: "when adverse events happened to LEP patients, about half caused physical harm, versus 30% for English speakers." Do not say "LEP patients have twice as many adverse events."

**5.4 Counterweight: across 26 studies, LEP was not consistently linked to worse hospital outcomes.**
- Figure: "Of 16 studies examining LEP and all-cause mortality, 13 found no significant association. Of 17 studies measuring LOS, 9 found no difference, 4 found longer LOS, 3 found shorter LOS." Readmissions were higher "in the setting of chronic medical conditions such as heart failure," not after surgery or acute illness. "Five studies evaluated complications or harm ... and no differences were found."
- Source: Woods AP et al. Limited English Proficiency and Clinical Outcomes After Hospital-Based Care in English-Speaking Countries: a Systematic Review. J Gen Intern Med 2022;37(8):2050-2061. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35112283/
- Strength: strong as a review. Know this before a judge asks. The defensible pitch is about understanding and access, plus the Karliner result, not a claim that language alone always worsens outcomes.
- Related single study: Manuel SP et al. JAMA Netw Open 2022;5(3):e221842. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8914571/ 4,721 joint replacement patients; LEP linked to longer stay (IRR 1.15, 95% CI 1.07-1.25) and higher cost (IRR 1.08), but "not with 30-day readmission (odds ratio, 0.80; 95% CI, 0.49-1.28)."

**5.5 Clinicians often skip professional interpreters on the ward.**
- Figure: "71% reported using professional interpreters for fewer than 60% of hospital encounters." "Most (91%) perceived their quality of communication with hospitalized LEP patients as worse."
- Source: Tang AS et al. J Health Care Poor Underserved 2014;25(4):1784-1798. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25418242/
- n: 149 resident physicians (73% response), one US academic institution.
- Strength: weak to moderate (self-report survey).

**5.6 US population with limited English.**
- Figure: "the share of the U.S. population who spoke English less than 'very well' decreased from 9 percent to 8 percent" (2009 to 2019, population aged 5+). 67.8 million people spoke a language other than English at home in 2019.
- Source: Dietrich S, Hernandez E. Language Use in the United States: 2019. American Community Survey Reports ACS-50. US Census Bureau, August 2022. https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/publications/2022/acs/acs-50.pdf
- Strength: strong (ACS, about 3 million housing units).
- Count in people: Karliner 2017 states "Twenty-five million people in the United States have limited English proficiency." NOT VERIFIED from Census directly for 2023 or 2024 (the Census API now needs a key; secondary sites cite about 29.6 million but I did not confirm it).

**5.7 Italy: 5.56 million foreign citizens, 9.4% of residents (1 Jan 2026).**
- Figure: "Al 1° gennaio 2026 la popolazione residente di cittadinanza straniera è pari a 5 milioni e 560mila unità, in aumento di 188mila individui (+3,5%) rispetto all'anno precedente, con un'incidenza sulla popolazione totale del 9,4%." Total residents 58.943 million.
- Source: ISTAT, Indicatori demografici, Anno 2025 (published March 2026). https://www.istat.it/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Report_Indicatori-demografici_Anno-2025.pdf
- Strength: strong (national statistics office). Note: this counts foreign citizenship, not foreign birth or language. Many foreign citizens speak Italian well, and many naturalised citizens (196,000 acquisitions in 2025 alone) may not. Phrase it as "foreign residents," not "people who don't speak Italian."
- NOT VERIFIED: foreign-born share of Italy's population; figures for Liguria or the province of Imperia.

**5.8 Italy ED: language barriers in a quarter of immigrant patient visits (Parma).**
- Figure: significant linguistic barriers "recorded in nearly one fourth of the patients (24.8%)," rising to 58.3% in patients aged 60+ and 43.8% in red or yellow triage codes. A translating intermediary was present for 24.9% of patients. A 24-hour telephone translation service existed but its use was not reported.
- Source: Numeroso F et al. Different perception of communication barriers with immigrants in the emergency department: a survey of emergency physicians. World J Emerg Med 2015;6(2):111-117. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4458470/
- n: 479 questionnaires about immigrant patient visits, completed by 21 emergency physicians, University Hospital of Parma, 2011.
- Strength: weak (physician perception, old, single site). The only quantified Italian hospital data I could open.
- NOT VERIFIED: national data on the availability or cost of "mediatori linguistico-culturali" in Italian hospitals. I found only local projects (for example a pilot at ASST Fatebenefratelli Sacco, Milan, with Emergency and Fondazione Arca, reported in Il Giorno) and theses. No Ministry of Health, AGENAS or ISS figure turned up.

**5.9 India (optional): not researched in depth.** NOT VERIFIED; no figures in this file.

## 6. Health literacy

**6.1 US: 36% of adults have basic or below-basic health literacy.**
- Figure: "The majority of adults (53 percent) had Intermediate health literacy. An additional 12 percent of adults had Proficient health literacy. Among the remaining adults, 22 percent had Basic health literacy, and 14 percent had Below Basic health literacy." (22 + 14 = 36%.)
- Source: Kutner M et al. The Health Literacy of America's Adults: Results From the 2003 National Assessment of Adult Literacy. NCES 2006-483. https://nces.ed.gov/pubs2006/2006483.pdf
- n: "more than 19,000 adults (ages 16 and older)."
- Strength: strong method, old data (2003; never repeated as a health scale). Say "2003 national assessment."

**6.2 Europe: limited health literacy ranges from 25% to 72% across 17 countries (HLS19).**
- Figure: across countries "about 33% had a 'problematic' level and for 13% it was 'inadequate'." Combined as limited health literacy, "with a range of 25% to 72%. That means that between one in four and three out of four residents in countries participating in the HLS19 have limited General HL."
- Source: HLS19 Consortium of the WHO Action Network M-POHL. HLS19 International Report, Executive Summary. Austrian National Public Health Institute (GÖG), 2021. https://m-pohl.net/sites/m-pohl.net/files/inline-files/HLS19%20Executive%20Summary%20V1.1.pdf
- n: 42,445 respondents in 17 countries; Italy 3,500. Data collected Nov 2019 to Jun 2021.
- Strength: strong (multi-country, standard instrument).

**6.3 Italy: about 58% have limited health literacy, well above the 17-country average.**
- Figure: Italy "23% di persone con livello di Health Literacy 'inadeguato', 35% 'problematico'", "34% 'sufficiente' e 9% 'eccellente'". The 17-country averages were "13%, 32%, 40% e 15%."
- Source: Quotidiano Sanità, 17 Dec 2021, reporting the HLS19 international report (WHO Europe / M-POHL). https://www.quotidianosanita.it/?p=112189 . A peer-reviewed South Tyrol paper gives the same figure: "57.7% of adults reporting limited health literacy according to HLS19." https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12386081/
- Strength: moderate (reputable secondary, consistent across two sources; the Italian national figure is not in the executive summary I opened). Before a slide, ideally confirm in the full HLS19 international report country table.

## 7. Nurse time and nursing shortage

**7.1 Nurses spend a median 22.5 minutes with the patient on discharge day, mostly on logistics.**
- Figure: "Median (IQR) total time spent with patients on the day of discharge by interns, senior residents, attending physicians, and nurses was 4.0 (0.75-6.0), 1.0 (0-2.0), 3.0 (0.5-7.0), and 22.5 (15.5-30.0) minutes, respectively. Most of the time was spent discussing logistics rather than discharge education."
- Source: Trivedi SP et al. JAMA Intern Med 2023 (see 2.5). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36939674/
- n: 33 patients.
- Strength: moderate. This is total bedside time, not education time. Don't call it "22 minutes of teaching."
- ED comparison: EM-TeBa (2.1) timed a regular ED discharge interview at 3:11 minutes on average.

**7.2 Italy has 6.9 practising nurses per 1,000 people, over 20% below the EU average.**
- Figure: "the density of practising nurses was 6.9 per 1 000 population, over 20 % below the EU average of 8.4, resulting in a nurse-to-doctor ratio of just 1.3, among the lowest in the EU." Nursing graduate numbers fell "by more than 3 % per year between 2013 and 2022." Italian nurses are paid roughly at the national average wage, while "nurses in most EU countries earn around 20 % more."
- Source: OECD / European Observatory on Health Systems and Policies. State of Health in the EU: Italy Country Health Profile 2025 (Dec 2025). https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/d0c754aa-4abf-49d5-b5c7-8d2adc634e18_en?filename=2025_chp_it_english.pdf
- Strength: strong (2023 data). The OECD Health at a Glance 2025 Italy note gives the same 6.9 against an OECD average of 9.2.

**7.3 Global nursing shortage: 5.8 million in 2023.**
- Figure: "The global nursing workforce has grown from 27.9 million in 2018 to 29.8 million in 2023" and the shortage fell "from 6.2 million in 2020 to 5.8 million in 2023," projected to 4.1 million by 2030. In "20 countries" (mostly high-income), "retirements are expected to outpace new entrants."
- Source: WHO news release on State of the World's Nursing 2025, 12 May 2025. https://www.who.int/news/item/11-05-2025-nursing-workforce-grows--but-inequities-threaten-global-health-goals
- Strength: strong. Note about 70% of the gap is in Africa and the Eastern Mediterranean, so for Italy use 7.2 instead.

## 8. Prior art: AI simplification, translation and automated teach-back

These justify two design choices: the fact lock (code checks every dose and date) and nurse review.

**8.1 Google Translate on ED discharge instructions: 2% (Spanish) and 8% (Chinese) of sentences carried potential for significant harm.**
- Figure: of 647 sentences, accurate in Spanish "594 (92%)" and Chinese "522 (81%)." Inaccurate translations with potential for clinically significant harm: Spanish 15 of 53 inaccurate (28%), equal to 2% of all sentences; Chinese 50 of 125 inaccurate (40%), equal to 8% of all sentences. Authors advise keeping English instructions alongside and a machine-translation warning.
- Source: Khoong EC et al. Assessing the Use of Google Translate for Spanish and Chinese Translations of Emergency Department Discharge Instructions. JAMA Intern Med 2019;179(4):580-582. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6450297/
- n: 100 sets of instructions, 647 sentences, professional translator evaluation.
- Strength: moderate (research letter, two languages). Key point: error rates differ a lot by language.

**8.2 Seven languages: meaning kept in 94% of Spanish translations but only 55% of Armenian.**
- Figure: "The overall meaning was retained for 82.5% (330/400) of the translations. Spanish had the highest accuracy rate (94%), followed by Tagalog (90%), Korean (82.5%), Chinese (81.7%), Farsi (67.5%), and Armenian (55%)." Conclusion: "should not be relied on for patient instructions."
- Source: Taira BR et al. A Pragmatic Assessment of Google Translate for Emergency Department Instructions. J Gen Intern Med 2021;36(11):3361-3365. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33674922/
- n: 400 translated statements (20 phrases x 7 languages), 20 native-speaker raters.
- Strength: moderate.

**8.3 2025: AI translation of real pediatric discharge instructions matched professionals in Spanish but was worse in Chinese, Vietnamese and Somali.**
- Figure: across all four languages, AI error-severity score 3.53 vs 4.48 for professional translations (difference 0.95; 95% CI 0.85-1.06). "The Chinese, Vietnamese, and Somali AI translations were inferior to the professional translations across all metrics, with the greatest differences for Somali."
- Source: Martos M et al. Accuracy of Artificial Intelligence vs Professionally Translated Discharge Instructions. JAMA Netw Open 2025;8(9):e2532312. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40960827/
- n: 148 sections from 34 real discharge instructions, Azure AI translator, 2 professional raters per language.
- Strength: moderate to strong (real issued instructions, blinded raters). The strongest recent evidence that machine translation of discharge instructions can't go unchecked, especially outside major languages.

**8.4 LLMs (ChatGPT) also make more clinically significant errors in less common languages.**
- Figure: for Haitian Creole, "ChatGPT (33.3%, P < .001) and Google Translate (23.3%, P = .024) contained more potentially clinically significant errors (severity score ≤3) ... than professional translations (8.3%)." Spanish and Portuguese were comparable to professionals.
- Source: Brewster RCL et al. Performance of ChatGPT and Google Translate for Pediatric Discharge Instruction Translation. Pediatrics 2024;154(1):e2023065573. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38860299/
- n: 20 standardised instructions x 3 languages.
- Strength: moderate.
- Also: Ray M et al. JAMA Pediatr 2025;179(9):1026-1033 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40622720/): GPT-4o Spanish translations of 20 pediatric instruction files were equivalent to professionals (mean difference 1.6 MQM points), but authors say "human review of LLM translation remains essential." Wu E et al. West J Emerg Med 2026;27(3):651-658 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42258871/): Copilot and Google Translate "produced a total of five potentially harmful translation errors, whereas none were identified for ChatGPT-4" (Albanian, Brazilian Portuguese, Vietnamese).

**8.5 LLM rewrites of discharge summaries are far more readable but omit or invent things.**
- Figure: grade level fell from 11.0 to 6.2; understandability (PEMAT) rose from 13% to 81%. But only "54 of 100 reviews (54.0%)" gave the best accuracy rating, summaries were "rated entirely complete in 56 reviews (56.0%)," and "Eighteen reviews noted safety concerns, mostly involving omissions, but also several inaccurate statements (termed hallucinations)."
- Source: Zaretsky J et al. Generative Artificial Intelligence to Transform Inpatient Discharge Summaries to Patient-Friendly Language and Format. JAMA Netw Open 2024;7(3):e240357. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38466307/
- n: 50 discharge summaries, NYU Langone, GPT-4 via Azure, 2 physician reviewers each.
- Strength: moderate. This is the single best citation for the fact lock: readability gains are real, and so are omissions. Safe line: "In a 2024 JAMA Network Open study, 18 of 100 physician reviews of GPT-4 patient-friendly discharge summaries flagged safety concerns, mostly omissions."

**8.6 LLM discharge narratives: same quality as physicians, more errors.**
- Figure: LLM narratives had "2.91 [2.54]" unique errors per summary vs "1.82 [1.94]" for physicians; overall harm low for both, with "only 1 LLM-generated narrative (compared with 0 physician-generated narratives) scoring 4 or greater" (potential for permanent harm).
- Source: Williams CYK et al. Physician- and Large Language Model-Generated Hospital Discharge Summaries. JAMA Intern Med 2025;185(7):818-825. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40323616/
- n: 100 encounters, 22 attending reviewers, UCSF.
- Strength: moderate to strong.

**8.7 RCT (Germany, 2026): LLM patient-oriented discharge summaries raised patient activation.**
- Figure: "At discharge, the intervention group had significantly higher PAM-13 scores than controls (Hodges-Lehmann median of differences 9·6 points [95% CI 4·3-15·1], p=0·0009)." GPT-4o output was reviewed by the treating physician.
- Source: Rust P et al. Lancet Digit Health 2026. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.landig.2026.100991 (PubMed 42150962)
- n: 128 randomised (64 vs 64), Helios University Hospital Wuppertal.
- Strength: moderate (RCT, small, single centre). Shows the European clinical appetite. The authors flag "data privacy" as a barrier to routine adoption.

**8.8 Closest prior art to automated teach-back: the "virtual nurse" (2009) used multiple-choice checks and alerted a human nurse.**
- Description: an animated agent at the bedside taught the discharge plan; "We also used short 'open book' comprehension tests during the interaction to test patient understanding of key points with a few multiple-choice questions." If a patient failed again, "an alert was posted for a human nurse to follow up with them." Most low-literacy patients "said they preferred receiving the discharge information from the agent over their doctor or nurse."
- Source: Bickmore TW, Pfeifer LM, Jack BW. Taking the Time to Care: Empowering Low Health Literacy Hospital Patients with Virtual Nurse Agents. CHI 2009. https://khoury.northeastern.edu/home/laurap/pub/CHI09.VirtualNurse.pdf
- Strength: design study with user testing (small samples). Relevant for originality: the idea of an automated check plus nurse alert is not new. What Homeward adds is open, spoken answers in the patient's own language graded fact by fact, a translated rendering for the nurse, and code-checked doses and dates. Say this plainly if asked.
- Other activity, NOT VERIFIED: UMass Chan Medical School describes an AI tool for diabetes medication safety that asks patients to explain medication changes back (page blocked by Cloudflare from here; described only in a search snippet). Vendor blogs (Simbo AI, Simbie AI) claim AI post-discharge calls with teach-back; marketing only, no outcome data. I found no peer-reviewed evaluation of an LLM that grades spoken teach-back at discharge.

## 9. Privacy rules that favour on-premise AI

**9.1 GDPR Article 9: health data is a special category; processing is prohibited unless an exception applies.**
- Quote: "Processing of personal data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, or trade union membership, and the processing of genetic data, biometric data for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person, data concerning health or data concerning a natural person's sex life or sexual orientation shall be prohibited." Article 9(2)(h) allows processing for medical diagnosis, health or social care under EU or member-state law, with the professional-secrecy safeguard of 9(3).
- Source: Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Article 9 (text via gdpr-info.eu). https://gdpr-info.eu/art-9-gdpr/
- Strength: primary law. Also note Homeward's audio of a patient's voice adds sensitivity.

**9.2 Italian Garante decalogo on AI in the national health service (Oct 2023): human oversight, data quality, integrity, privacy by design.**
- Content (Italian, from the PDF): section "9. Supervisione umana" (human oversight); "6. Qualità dei dati" requires data that are "esatti e, se necessario, aggiornati"; "7. Integrità e riservatezza" restates the GDPR duty of "adeguata sicurezza"; privacy by design under Article 25 means "nella realizzazione di sistemi di intelligenza artificiale in ambito sanitario devono essere adottate misure tecniche e organizzative" adequate to protect patients. Key principles reported widely: transparency, human-supervised automated decisions, non-discrimination.
- Source: Garante per la protezione dei dati personali. Decalogo per la realizzazione di servizi sanitari nazionali attraverso sistemi di Intelligenza Artificiale. Doc-web 9938038, 10 Oct 2023. https://www.garanteprivacy.it/home/docweb/-/docweb-display/docweb/9938038
- Strength: primary regulator guidance. The decalogo does not say "AI must run on-premise." Fair framing: it demands human oversight (Homeward's nurse confirms facts and gets the uncertain grades) and data security and minimisation, which a model running inside the hospital makes far easier to show.
- Context (secondary, Italian financial press, Dec 2024): the Garante fined OpenAI 15 million euros over ChatGPT's GDPR compliance (order of 2 Nov 2024; a Rome court suspended the fine pending appeal in March 2025). Shows the Italian regulator acts on cloud LLMs. I read news coverage only, not the order itself.

**9.3 HIPAA (US): any cloud provider that processes patient data is a business associate, even with encrypted data.**
- Quote: "When a covered entity engages the services of a CSP to create, receive, maintain, or transmit ePHI ... the CSP is a business associate under HIPAA." "This is true even if the CSP processes or stores only encrypted ePHI and lacks an encryption key for the data." The parties "must enter into a HIPAA-compliant business associate agreement (BAA)." A CSP is generally not a mere "conduit."
- Source: HHS Office for Civil Rights, Guidance on HIPAA and Cloud Computing. https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/special-topics/health-information-technology/cloud-computing/index.html (live site blocked automated access; read via the Internet Archive copy).
- Strength: primary guidance. Fair framing: cloud AI is allowed with a BAA, but every API vendor becomes a regulated party. A model on the hospital's own GPU removes that link.
- Note: idea.md says AMD Token Factory's terms forbid health data. That was not checked in this research pass.

## Top 8 numbers for the pitch

Picked for being defensible first and striking second. Each has a safe slide phrasing.

1. **78% / 20%** (Engel 2009, n=140, US EDs). Slide: "78% of ED patients had a gap in understanding at least one part of their discharge, and when they had one, they noticed it only 1 time in 5."

2. **81%** (Ziaeian 2012, n=377, older inpatients). Slide: "81% of older patients either received a medication error at discharge or didn't understand at least one change to their medicines."

3. **1 in 33** (Trivedi 2023, JAMA Intern Med, 33 patients observed all day). Slide: "Watched from 7 am to discharge, only 1 of 33 patients was asked to explain the plan back."

4. **49% to 11.9% in 1:39 minutes** (Mahajan 2020, EM-TeBa, n=483, Netherlands). Slide: "Teach-back cut the share of ED patients leaving with a comprehension gap from 49% to 12%, in under two minutes."

5. **17.8% to 13.4%** (Karliner 2017, 1,963 LEP discharges, one US hospital). Slide: "When limited-English patients got easy interpreter access at the bedside, 30-day readmissions fell from 17.8% to 13.4%, and rose again when access faded."

6. **18 of 100 reviews flagged safety concerns** (Zaretsky 2024, JAMA Netw Open, GPT-4, 50 summaries) plus **8% of sentences with potential for harm** (Khoong 2019, Google Translate, Chinese). Slide: "AI rewrites are more readable but drop things: physicians flagged safety concerns, mostly omissions, in 18 of 100 reviews. That's why Homeward code-checks every dose and date."

7. **3.85 million readmissions, $16,300 each; 2,334 hospitals penalised** (HCUP SB304, 2020 data; CMS FY2027 via Healthcare Dive). Slide: "US hospitals saw 3.85 million 30-day readmissions in 2020 at $16,300 on average, and Medicare is cutting pay at 80% of hospitals for excess readmissions this year."

8. **Italy: 9.4% foreign residents, about 58% limited health literacy, 6.9 nurses per 1,000** (ISTAT Jan 2026; HLS19 via Quotidiano Sanità; OECD 2025). Slide: "In Italy, 1 resident in 11 is a foreign citizen, more than half of adults have limited health literacy, and nurse numbers are over 20% below the EU average."

Reserve: Forster 2003, "19% of medical patients had an adverse event within weeks of going home, two thirds from drugs" (n=400). Vick 2026 is the caveat to know for Q&A: teach-back improves adherence, but trials show no clear effect on knowledge scores. Homeward's pitch rests on catching the specific misunderstanding for the nurse, not on teach-back raising test scores.
